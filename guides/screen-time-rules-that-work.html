Screens Ages 3–12

Screen-time rules that work (and survive the weekend)

A budget, not a ban. What the guidelines actually say by age, why quality beats quantity, and how to set limits kids accept.

By the BetterKid Editorial Team·8 min read·Updated Sep 2026✔ Reviewed against current paediatric guidance
In this guide
  1. What the guidelines say
  2. Budget, don't ban
  3. The 5 rules that hold
  4. Phones and first devices

What the guidelines actually say

The American Academy of Pediatrics no longer publishes a single hours number for kids over 5. Their guidance: under 18 months, avoid except video chat; 18–24 months, high-quality programming watched together; 2–5 years, about 1 hour/day of quality content, co-viewed; 6+, consistent limits that protect sleep, physical activity and face-to-face time. The WHO adds: under-5s should not be sedentary in front of a screen for more than an hour a day.

What the research consistently shows is that displacement is the problem, not the pixels: screens hurt when they replace sleep, play, reading and conversation. That reframes your job from “police the minutes” to “protect the must-dos”.

Budget, don't ban

Start from 24 hours. Subtract sleep (9–12 h for 6–12s), school, meals, homework, activities and outdoor play. What's left is discretionary time — and recreational screens should take about a third of it, capped by age. Our Screen-Time Budget tool does this in 30 seconds and produces a visual your child can understand. Kids accept limits they can see.

The 5 rules that hold

  1. Must-dos first. Screens come after homework, movement and chores — automatically, every day, no negotiation.
  2. No screens in bedrooms overnight. This single rule has the largest measured effect on sleep and mood. Devices charge in the kitchen.
  3. Screen-free meals. For everyone. Parental phone use at the table predicts children's phone use more than any rule.
  4. Content over clock. An hour building in Minecraft with a friend is not an hour of autoplay shorts. Distinguish creating, connecting and consuming.
  5. Write it down. A signed family media agreement cuts arguments because the rule — not the parent — is the authority.

Phones and first devices

Median age of first smartphone in North America is now about 11. There's no magic age; there is a readiness test: can they follow existing screen rules without policing, lose a privilege gracefully, and tell you when something online feels wrong? Start with a “dumb” phone or a watch, and layer capabilities as trust is earned. Set parental controls before handing it over — retro-fitting them is a fight.

Key takeaways

  • Guidelines: ~1 h quality content at 2–5; consistent limits after 6.
  • Displacement, not screens, is the harm — protect sleep, play, reading.
  • Devices out of bedrooms overnight is the highest-impact rule.
  • Use a written agreement so the rule is the authority, not you.
